Later Summer Birding in North Norfolk
=====================================

.. articleMetaData::
   :Where: London, UK
   :Date: 2026-10-12 16:00 Europe/London
   :Tags: photography, birds
   :Short: north-norfolk

Some of you might have picked up that I have started to show more interest in
birds. Either on the side panel of this blog, on Mastodon, or in Real Life™.

One time I was visiting `RSPB Rainham Marshes
<https://www.rspb.org.uk/days-out/reserves/rainham-marshes>`_, I spoke with a
chap who recommended I should go visit the reserves in North Norfolk. I was
aware of the richness of habits there from the `BBC's Springwatch
<https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b007qgm3>`_  and Autumnwatch programs,
including the conflagration of large flocks of birds there in the autumn
migration season, especially at spring high tide.

I had done a little research, but found it hard to decide on where I should
go, until I discovered that I could do an organised tour of the area. So I
ended up on a train to King's Lynn in mid-September to join a `tour with
Wildlife Worldwide
<https://www.wildlifeworldwide.com/group-tours/norfolk-in-late-summer>`_.

----

**Day 1: Settling In and Our First Outings**

No story about going anywhere in the UK is complete without some public travel
trouble. First the useful tube line was suspended, and secondly the train into
King's Lynn was delayed due to speed restrictions.

At the station, me, and two others of the group were met by our tour guide,
Harry, and transferred to the Knights Hill hotel, where we would be staying for
the whole trip.

As we were a little too early for check-in, the three of us had a light lunch
at the associated pub. After check-in, we met the other four of our group, and
had a briefing to find out what the general plan was for the next few days.
What species did we want to see? I was keen on seeing a Bittern, Richard a
Green Woodpecker, and Mary a Hare.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b02-norfolk-thornham-harbour/d54_9309_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Herring Gull on a Post
   :align: left
   :width: 33%

The good news: Lots of places to visit, with different habitats and species.
The bad news: Some days we had to get up at 5:30am.

After the briefing, we headed out to our first spot, `Holme Dunes
<https://www.norfolkwildlifetrust.org.uk/HolmeDunes>`_. This was our first
look at the coast, where we saw Cormorants and a flock of Red Knots — way out on
the sea. The shoreline had a few Herring Gulls and Oystercatchers wading
about. A little further out on the broad walk, I was quite excited to see a
raptor, which upon closer identification turned out to be a gull. After
making it back to the car park just before closing time, we spotted two
Buzzards flying, and a Sparrowhawk perched on a post.

Our next stop, Thornham Harbour, is a little further east along the coast. On
the shore here were many gulls, and a few Spoonbills with their heads tucked
in. In the distance, a Marsh Harrier was perched near the beach, a Kestrel
hovered overhead, and a small flock of Greylag Geese was doing wherever geese
do. The typical "Herring Gull on a post" rounded up the evening.

Back in the hotel we had dinner, and went through a list of all the birds we
saw that day: listing them.

----

**Day 2: Around Cley Marshes and Kelling Heath**

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b03-norfolk-blakeney/d54_9404_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Bearded Tit in the Reeds
   :align: left
   :width: 50%

The big outing on the second day would be `NWT Cley Marshes
<https://www.norfolkwildlifetrust.org.uk/CleyMarshes>`_, a breeding bird
sanctuary. But it wouldn't be the first stop of the day, as we were too early.

Instead, we had a wander around just west of the town, near Cley's windmill.
On the walk towards the sea, we had a glimpse of a Bearded Tit. A bird that I
had heard a few times, but had never seen. It was hiding in the reeds, but it
did show long enough for a distant photo.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b03-norfolk-blakeney/d54_9594_blog.jpg
   :title: Flock of Spoonbills
   :align: right
   :width: 50%

A little further out, I thought I had spotted a goose. But when Mike had a
look with his binoculars, it turned out there were two birds there. And
neither of them was a goose. Instead, we had found two Curlews, the first of
our trip, and the first one I had ever seen. A flock of Pink-footed Goose came
flying over once we neared our car. One of the first to come through the area
on their yearly migration south.

After a few minutes travel, we arrived at Cley Marshes. We got our `Norfolk
Wildlife Trust <https://www.norfolkwildlifetrust.org.uk/>`_ membership packs,
and headed out to the first set of hides. A flock of Spoonbills promptly flew
over, followed by a flock of Golden Plovers.

From the hides we could see many birds on the islands of Pat's Pool, mostly
Greylag Geese and Spoonbills, but also different types of Gulls.

In the shallow water, Pied Avocet and Ruff were foraging for grubs, and a
Sandwich Tern flew overhead trying to figure out whether to try to catch a
fish. But most of the birds were quite far away to make them out clearly.

After enjoying the view, we moved on from the central hides to Bishop's Hide,
which looks at the lake from the other side, and a slightly closer distance.
From here we spotted some more species too. Some ducks, a Greater Black-backed
Gull, Black-tailed Godwits, and a Lapwing. Flocks of Golden Plovers and Greylag
Geese adorned the skies as well.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b04-norfolk-cley-marshes/d55_0769_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Gulls and Ducks

Before we went for lunch, we had a short drive to the East Bank. This raised
dyke gave great views over the Marshes. We saw many species again, but
binoculars, or a telescope was required to see them well. Some heather still
had some nice colour to it, and in it, we saw this Curlew.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b05-norfolk-cley-marshes-east-bank/d55_1130_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Curlew in the Heather

At the far end of the walk, on the beach, we spotted more Terns and a Scoter,
quite unexpectedly. On photos later, I also found a Bar-tailed Godwit, and a
Ring Necked Plover, both new species to me.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b06-norfolk-kelling-heath/d55_1472_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Dartford Warbler
   :align: right
   :width: 50%

After lunch, we headed to Kelling Heath, with the intention of hopefully
seeing some rarer birds. Kelling Heath is one of the few places in Norfolk
where Dartford Warblers breed. On the way upwards to the heath, we saw flocks
of Linnet, a Robin singing for us, and a few Wood Pigeons. A Muntjac was
quietly grazing on the grass.

On the heath itself, we spotted a few Stonechats, and one of them was kind
enough to sit still for a while. Collins Bird Guide says about Dartford
Warbles: "often reveals itself in the top of a bush", which is exactly were we
found this tiny juvenile a few minutes later.

Back at the hotel, we had an interesting discussion about why some bird names
get the prefix "Common", and some others "European". I don't think we got to a
conclusion though!

**Day 3: Titchwell Marsh, Brancaster Staite, and Holkham**

We enjoyed a reasonable early breakfast and headed to `RSPB Titchwell Marsh
<https://www.rspb.org.uk/days-out/reserves/titchwell-marsh>`_, one of the most
well-known reserves in the country. As habitats, it covers reed beds,
woodlands, fresh- and salt-water marshes, as well as a beach.

We started at the car-park, where we heard a Treecreeper, but could not spot
it. Instead, we set of on our walk towards the Island Hide, where we saw our
first bird, a Curlew. Harry, our guide had heard that a rare bird — a Spotted
Crake — was spotted in the reed beds in the last few days, and expected that
it was going to get busy later in the morning with people wanting to see it.
The hide was indeed busy, but most of us could find a spot to sit or stand.

During the next hour and a half we looked at several types of waders, coming
close by the hide. Much closer than at Cley Marshes.

After much searching, and waiting, we did finally see the rare Spotted Crake
through our guide's scope — although I was frustrated that it took me so much
longer to find it than many others. Giving directions and hints as to where to
see certain animals among reed beds is a skill. I didn't think I had a photo
of it, but there was a blurry shot of it on my reel.

To be honest, although it was fun to find such a rarity, I had more fun in
observing the many other species that were on the lake and the marsh on the
other side of the hide. We had particularly good views of Black-tailed
Godwits, Pied Avocets, and a Common Snipe was foraging right in from of us. On
the island in the marsh there were flocks of Spoonbills and Greylag Geese
coming in for a landing.

A while later, we moved onto visiting the next hide, the Parrinder Hide. The
area upon which this hide looks out over is fairly different, with quite a lot
of mud. This attracts other kinds of species. There were a lot of them quite
far away, which makes them hard to see, especially because it was against the
Sun. Closer by, we did watch Green Sandpipers and Ruffs probing the mud for
grubs again.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b07-norfolk-titchwell-marsh/d55_2782_blog.jpg
   :title: Turnstone Turning Over Stones
   :align: left
   :width: 50%

Away from the hides, the atmosphere on the beach was very different. It wasn't
busy at all, but it was *very* windy. When we arrived, there was a large flock
of Spoonbills flying over — perhaps the same one we saw earlier.

On the ground, there were several species, such as Herring Gulls, Black-headed
Gulls, and Oystercatchers. My favourite behaviour was that of the Ruddy
Turnstone, turning over stones on the beach to find food.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b07-norfolk-titchwell-marsh/d55_2736_blog_1.jpg
   :title: An Oystercatcher, several Turnstones, and a Herring Gull

We did briefly go back to the Parrinder hide as we had got word that there
were some other rarer species, such as Little Stints. They might have been
there in the distance, but I found it very hard to see anything at all, with
the sun glaring on the water. Perhaps I should invest in a spotting scope at
some point. However, I did get a nice photo of a Black-tailed Godwit strutting
about, after it was chased off by another bird.

On the way back to the car park, I found the row of twitchers, hoping to spot
the rare Spotted Crake amusing. Some of them had travelled from quite far
away. Don't get me wrong, I like watching birds, but I am more interested in
their behaviour than "listing". Learning their behaviour, so I can take better
photos is much more satisfying than that.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b07-norfolk-titchwell-marsh/d55_2904_blog.jpg
   :title: Twitchers

I could easily have spent another day at this wonderful reserve — we skipped
quite a few sections and hides — but there were other places to visit as well.

After lunch, we headed to `Brancaster Staite
<https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/norfolk/brancaster-estate/brancaster-staithe-harbour>`_
and its little harbour. The harbour is mostly a muddy flat, which turned out
to be excellent for observing waders. As soon as we got there, a Curlew was
perched right upon a bank of sand, next to a Herring Gull chick begging food.
A Common Redshank was foraging for food.

Out of sheer luck, we spotted a Kingfisher in the reeds too!

But my favourite time here was a little further away on the mud flat to see
the Redshanks and Godwits foraging, amongst small Ringed Plovers and Ruffs. I
hadn't really realised how small plovers actually are.

I think my best photo of the day was this Red Shank, literally walking up to
us.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b08-norfolk-brancaster-staithe/d55_3859_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Redshank Strutting

Before we headed back to the hotel, we had one more location to visit,
`Holkham <https://www.holkham.co.uk/visit/holkham-beach-nature-reserve/>`_. We
first drove into the park, as Green Woodpeckers are often seen there, but we
had no luck, and had to do with hundreds of deer, and dozens of Mistle
Thrushes, instead.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b09-norfolk-holkham/d55_4137_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Hare
   :align: left
   :width: 50%

After our flying visit in the park, we headed up Lady Anne's Drive with the
intention to park up at the end, and have a look around the marshes on either
side for raptors, and other species.

However, when doing so, we spotted this hare munching around. It had very much
seen us, but didn't seem to be bothered. This made Mary very happy. After
about fifteen minutes we left it on its own, and walked to the coast with its
mudflats. We didn't see any new species there, or really nothing much at all.

When we were done, we headed to the hotel for our evening dinner, and the
daily listing. We had an early night, as the next two days we had to get up
really early™.

----

**Day 4: RSPB Snettisham and Local Places**

I woke up at 5am, to be ready for 6am, so that we could be at `RSPB Snettisham
<https://www.rspb.org.uk/days-out/reserves/snettisham>`_ by 6:30am. I am not a
morning person, so you might be wondering: Why‽

Arguably, the whole reason that I went on this trip is to experience the
Snettisham Spectacular. A few times a year, during spring tide in autumn and
winter, tens of thousands of Red Knots, Oystercatchers, Dunlins, and
Black-tailed Godwits, which are feeding on The Wash, are being pushed of these
mudflats. The high tide forces them off The Wash, and many of them are pushed
into a lagoon at the reserve to wait out the high tide, before they leave and
forage again on the great expanse.

So this is why I was finishing my bacon butty at 5:55am, and wolfing down my
cup of tea — getting ready to head in the dark to the reserve. On these days,
you must book a parking space, which our touring organisation had already
done.

When we got to the reserve around 6:30am, it was still dark, and raining. The
weather was definitely not great for the 30-minute walk to where we needed to
be. When walking south we could already see large flocks of birds in the sky,
and before we could get to the final viewing point. We were a little late it
seemed, with the migration into the lagoon already in progress. Sometimes, the
expectation on when high tide is, is a little off it seems. Luckily, by that
time, it also stopped raining, setting the scene for the Spectacular.

It is really hard to describe the twists and turns that large groups of birds
make, so instead of me trying to describe it with words, here are some images:

Once the birds come off The Wash, they settle in the lagoon. They arrive in a
maelstrom of movement, before settling down after ten minutes. There are so
many of them, making it hard to see where one bird starts and another ends.

There are also multiple species involved. Here we have mostly Red Knots in the
foreground, and a bunch of Black-tailed Godwits on the back of the little
island.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_4471_blog.jpg
   :title: Godwits and Red Knots — a lot of them

Beyond the Knots and Godwits, there is also a large colony of Spoonbills and
Cormorants in the lagoon. Here, you can see them all lined up, with a flock of
Oystercatchers on the distant bank.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_4548_blog.jpg
   :title: Spoonbills and Cormorants Lined Up

After admiring the large flocks coming over, I walked around the lagoon to one
of the hides. It was busy in there, but you got a great view of the thousands
of Oystercatchers that also made it here.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_4616_blog.jpg
   :title: Oystercatcher in the Lagoon

After a while, when the high tide is receding, the birds move away from the
lagoon and back into The Wash, reversing the spectacular displays from earlier
in the morning. As we were now in the right place at the right time, the
experience was even better, with the birds flying straight overhead, metres
away, and in their thousands.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_4787_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Red Knots at Touching Distance

After we finished admiring the spectacular, we walked back slowly towards the
car park, but not before having a good look to see what else we could see in
the woodlands, and marshes. We found (our first) Turtle Doves, and a
Black-bellied Plover on the marsh.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_5540_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Black-bellied Plover

Just before leaving the reserve, we also enjoyed watching this Goldfinch
eating the seeds from the plants. I had seen them before, but the light was
perfect to capture this encounter.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b10-norfolk-snettisham/d55_5609_blog.jpg
   :title: Goldfinch

We went for a light lunch at `Norfolk Lavender's Café
<https://norfolk-lavender.co.uk/>`_ and after that we retreated to the hotel
to rest for a while. I didn't quite find that necessary, and instead went to
the pub to have a first pass through my thousands of photos.

When everybody was well rested, we headed back out. The first location was the
Choseley Drying Barns, where we saw Red Legged Partridges, Pheasants, and a
few Red Kites soaring over.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b11-norfolk-choseley-drying-barns/d55_5779_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Red Kite

We continued to the Holme Dunes. There were some reports that Barn Owls had
been spotted just after sunset. We only stayed briefly, but didn't see any in
the short time we were there — it was also not after sunset.

Richard mentioned that a few years ago, he and Sheila had been to `West Newton
church <http://www.norfolkchurches.co.uk/westnewton/westnewton.htm>`_ , where
they had seen a Little Owl. But as it was now later in the day, he and a few
others wanted to get ready for dinner. After they were dropped off, a few of
us headed back out not wanting to laze about for an hour, and made the trip to
West Newton's church.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b12-norfolk-west-newton/d55_5798_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Green Woodpecker under a Tree

We did not see an owl, but we *did* see this lovely Green Woodpecker under a
tree — sorry Richard. We also had a chat with one of the church wardens, who
said that the owls had been displaced by Jackdaws this year, but these had now
left. She showed some lovely photos of two Little Owl chicks on a gravestone —
taken by a certain Les.

Back at the hotel, during dinner, we couldn't wait to show the photo of the
Green Woodpecker as part of listing all the species we had seen that day.

**Day 5: RSPB Snettisham, and Finishing with…**

We decided that, because we were a little late yesterday, get up at the same
time to be at Snettisham at the same time too. With the tide being high 40
minutes later than the day before, we should have a lot more time to get into
position. It was such a different morning — clear skies, and no rain to be
seen. The sunrise was amazing.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b13-norfolk-snettisham/d55_5812_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Sunrise

We were dropped off at the accessible parking location, were we had a chat
with one of the RSPB rangers. I had spotted that his name was "Les", and
wondered whether he was the Les that took the photos of the Little Owls that
the church warden showed. It turned out to be him indeed, and he informed us
that the Little Owls ought to be back at the church now. What a coincidence.

When we arrived at the viewing point, there were still many birds out on The
Wash. The tide wasn't very high at all, so there was no reason for the birds
to make their way into the lagoon yet.

But flocks of birds were in the air, nicely being caught by the low yellow
sunlight.

As it was a Monday morning, it was not nearly as busy as the day before, which

It is hard to describe how many birds there really were. Besides the 50
thousand Red Knots, the 8 thousand Oystercatchers, and the Godwits, there were

Once the event really got going, with the tide now approaching highest, and
the light just being right, the sight was preposterously amazing.

At some point, the Oystercatchers were flying overhead so closely, that it
felt like I could almost touch them.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b13-norfolk-snettisham/d55_6475_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Close-Up Oystercatcher

The lagoon became filled with birds again. Mostly the Red Knot, but also many
Oystercatchers, interspersed with Little Egrets. As we had a bit more time, it
allowed from some more exploration of the lagoon area.

After a few hours, we had seen enough at Snettisham. There were a few hours
left to explore until the scheduled tour finishing time, around midday.

First we headed out to `Hunstanton Cliffs
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunstanton_Cliffs>`_, in the hope to see some
Gannets and Skuas on their migration over the sea. We didn't see any of them,
but instead we briefly saw an Arctic Skua/Parasitic Jaeger chasing gulls to
see if it could steal some fish they'd caught. It didn't succeed, but it
but was again a new, and unexpected, species.

With about half an hour to spare, we returned to West Newton's church. With
the additional information and encouragement of Les and the church warden, we
were hoping to spot a Little Owl in the tower. To our great surprise, as well
as our guide's, the Little Owl was right where it was supposed to be,
well-spotted by Harry from our moving van. I still don't understand how he
spots and identifies birds so easily. I guess it's a skill to learn over time.

In any case, ending our tour with finding the Little Owl in the church tower
was such a great experience.

.. image:: https://s3.drck.me/derickrethans-images.s3.uk.io.cloud.ovh.net/2026-09b15-norfolk-west-newton/d55_7072_blog_1.jpg
   :title: Little Owl in the Tower

After returning to the hotel, we said our goodbyes. We were all travelling
back. I had to wait a bit longer as I booked a later train, giving me the
opportunity to go through many more photos before getting on the train home
back to London. The two hours that that journey took, was also spend by
looking at more of the marvellous photos that I took on this excellent trip.

I will be back to this part of Norfolk next autumn, but likely on my own
steam, as there are so many places that warranted longer stays or more
exploration. I have learned about good places too, which I will cherish for a
while.

Thanks Harry, Mike, Bob, Richard, Marie, Mary, Sheila, and Don, for the great
trip!
